For centuries or even millennia, religion acted as a central institution for system in human society. It anchored socio-moral values, ethics, and faith ensuring a systematic society. It also institutionalized the idea of God and established a set of rules that were believed to bring people closer to God (or the divine). However, many studies show that with the advancements in science, education, storage of verified knowledge, and information systems, the degree of religious faith among individuals and society has significantly declined. This suggests that religion and the idea of God were historically crucial to sustain societal structure, uniformity, and sustainability – like the legal system in the contemporary world. Nevertheless, with the passage of time, they have simply begun to lose their grip over people's mind and the society as a whole.

In recent times, the term "spirituality" has gained a huge popularity across the world, not merely in South Asia. Still, what is the meaning of spirituality at first? The answer is not singular, as everyone holds a subjective definition of it. This vagueness makes the question more worth asking.

Many key philosophers and psychologists suggest different definitions of spirituality. However, here, I would emphasize how people across the globe generally understand it these days. The Cambridge Dictionary shows, “spirituality is the quality that involves deep feelings and beliefs of a religious nature, rather than the physical parts of life."

This means that spirituality fundamentally focuses on the non-materialistic or inner aspects of life. It emphasizes on an individual's bond with the universe or their own "inner self". It encourages individuals to explore where they personally find meaning – unlike traditional religions which impose strict obligations, rites, and rituals to please God.

Before we draw any conclusions or make assumptions about spirituality, we must take some social and natural facts into accounts.

First, the credibility and relevance of traditional religion have largely collapsed in the past century (or are gradually collapsing) due to accessibility to education, information, and knowledge. The decline in the percentage of religious people in censuses in developed and some developing nations is arguably the most concrete evidence of it. The population of religiously unaffiliated people has risen from 1.6 billion people (23%) in 2010, to 1.9 billion (24%) in 2020.

Second, many people feel lonely making them seek validation, reassurance, and a sense of belonging. Both religion and spirituality provide the very psychological supplements to meet these needs.

Third, people naturally experience moments of aha, epiphanies, and awe – which are indeed genuine and deeply meaningful. Some genuine human feelings of awe and wonder were either punished or suppressed by rigid religious beliefs. They were often redirected to God (or to the divine) too. Following the fall of religion’s credibility, there are many people who feel lost in this transition period. Now, they have no place to worship, pray, or confess with complete faith which leaves them isolated. Religion had remained as a trusted institution to anchor their morality, ethics, behaviour, and emotions.

I personally find the modern idea of spirituality to be quite two-faced. The basic building blocks of both religion and spirituality are almost identical. As we know, spirituality claims to oppose dogmatic socio-communal order and obligations. But it has its own hierarchy of spiritual teachers (Gurus) – which could be understandable to some extent. Nonetheless, spirituality relies heavily on unfalsifiable claims. Spirituality has effectively turned into a profit-driven industry selling people back their own egos in sacred makeup.

Amidst all of this, the primary concern is that spirituality may simply be the economic rebirth of religion. Historically, religions served as vital drivers to the economy, redistributing wealth through institutionalized obligations (no matter how hypocritical). The modern spirituality industry operates on the exact same framework. It has commercialized a psychologically fundamental human need: the desire to belong.

People used to donate wealth to temples and churches; now, they pay for so-called "spiritual" workshops.
Even though spirituality projects an identity of open-accessibility, numerous commercial ideas hide beneath it. For instance, certifications based on rankings; pay higher – get more valuable certificate. Retreat pricing (neo-gurudakshina to the spiritual instructor) might be justifiable, but how can you ever justify the certification-based rankings which are determined by the amount you pay? It is similar to buying fake educational degree certificates. However, to prevent themselves from rational questions the Gurus simply compare you with a Musk Deer (Kastoori Mriga) and enlightenment or inner-peace to its own aroma. This way, they trap you inside a capsule of belief they create using such absurd, symbolic, and unscientific comparisons.

If a spirit or any kind of spiritual essence resides within every person, why is financial transaction required to get a direct access to it? Did Siddhartha Gautama have a Guru to gain enlightenment? Did Nanak? Did Osho? Did Mahaveer?

In this sense, spirituality replicates platforms like Spotify, Claude, Canva, etc. The common principle is offering a basic version to all the users but reserve the "authentic" experience for premium paying customers.
When we talk about spirituality, the word "spirit" also follows. Regarding this term, Carl Jung defined it as an autonomous, non-material psychological reality experienced as an active, dynamic force. He argued that it brings meaning, inspiration, and higher consciousness to the human mind. He perceived the spirit as a subjective psychological core rather than any kind of metaphysical entity. He also relates it to the psyche, the deep mental structures driving human behaviour. On the contrary, the so-called "spiritual leaders" push an absurdly unfalsifiable and metaphysical definition of the spirit. Oftentimes, they rob some natural and scientifically explainable emotional states like awe, aha, and epiphanies. They repackage simple human experiences into out-of-reality stories and fictions merely for commercial gain and socio-political influence.

Spirituality has acknowledged that external deities and authoritarian religions are losing credibility. Which is why, it has shifted its focus from the external to the internal also known as "the inner self." It has basically relocated the office of faith-based influence from an external God to an "internalized spirit" or "cosmic self".
By convincing followers that the entire universe resides within them, spiritual leaders present standard human emotions as evidence of cosmic truth. In my personal opinion, this shift can be far more dangerous than traditional religions because of two reasons. First, human ego could possibly take a much darker form when individuals begin to view themselves as "divine" or "chosen ones". Second, the spiritual leaders (Gurus) take the seat previously occupied by traditional God.

Rejecting an external God is relatively easy because God is defined as a separate entity from us which can neither be seen, heard, nor felt. On the other hand, rejecting the idea of an internal spirit feels extremely similar to denying one's own existence and presence in the universe.

Blind obedience to external authority like God might often indicate lack of rationality and critical thinking in an individual. Nevertheless, believing that you have the whole universe within yourself or you are the avatar of the universe itself may pose immeasurably higher intellectual, moral, and social risks. Moreover, when someone holding such beliefs does a morally unjustifiable act, the belief itself becomes a justification or excuse – just like Krishna having multiple love affairs in the name of “leela” just because he’s God.  Hence, there is no external moral, social, and ethical standard left to call wrong as wrong.

I personally find no distinction between people believing in such unfalsifiable and egoistic ideas and Raskolnikov (from Dostoyevsky's Crime and Punishment) in his early days when he firmly believed that he was not an ordinary man and people like him were morally exceptional. Later, driven by this belief, Raskolnikov ends up committing two murders.

When traditional religious institutions lost their grip over the society's power dynamics, "the old guards" of religion subtly remoulded religion into spirituality to retain their influence. Some argue that spirituality as a different practice was into existence long before the decline of religions started – which is absolutely true.  However, my simple question to them is, "Why has it become popular at this phase of our civilization?" "Why was it not popular back then?" The answer is simple – religions were already powerful so, they did not need spirituality back then to indoctrinate people and sustain their socio-political dominance and finances.

Following the decline of religions, “the old guards” refurbished the concept of spirituality and made it fancy. The distinction from traditional religions that made spirituality more popular is that this new replica is more private and self-centred. This could be dangerous. Another difference is that it is far less accountable because questioning yourself is very complex and difficult than questioning an external God or religious faith. Now matter what, spirituality has already been successful in maintaining a good financial revenue and authority over public faith.

While practices like mindfulness, expression of gratitude, and meditation might help to promote self-awareness, connection with nature, and peace of mind, they need not be bought through financial transactions like retreat pricing or any kind of subscription. I personally do not understand how people are drawn to services like "body-count reset rituals," where the so-called Gurus reset the number of sexual partners you've had back to zero. Such services do not directly contribute to promoting self-awareness and mindfulness – they are just ways of looting people financially, emotionally, and psychologically.

People who believe in real spirituality practice meditation, gratitude, or a sense of connection to something they feel is beyond themselves – without any Guru. They neither purchase any product nor believe in any metaphysical claim about being divine. This “non-commercial spiritual” practice is at least better than the previously mentioned practices where people only spend their time, money, and energy unnecessarily.

However, the irony of spirituality hasn’t yet ended yet. What people think is spiritual is actually biological and psychological rather "spiritual". The wonderful experiences you have during or after meditating or expressing gratitude is an outcome of your mind releasing neurochemicals like serotonin, dopamine, oxytocin, etc. These neurochemicals make you happy and feel at peace – similar to listening to a music you love or being with your loved ones. And when people are unaware of this process occurring in their brains – human behaviour plays a role to justify or explain it anyhow to us. Consequently, people relate this neurochemical process to a connection with "the divine" or something beyond themselves because they do not understand what is happening inside their brain. This is exactly where the tale of Musk Deer is introduced by the Gurus.

So, practicing meditation and gratitude doesn't need to be contemplated essentially as “spiritual” – but it is a matter of personal choice though. Following this, I also believe that many institutes teaching yoga, meditation, gratitude, and mindfulness should stop calling themselves anything like “spiritual wellness/recovery centre”. They can simply promote mental well-being in its naked and scientific form without using any “sacred” or “spiritual” packaging. Living with thoughts based on empirical facts could be challenging to many people yet it is incomparable to believing in metaphysical, fictional, and unfalsifiable ideas and living by them.

Essentially, what spirituality sells us is merely psychological as well as philosophical to some extent. Therefore, the existence of this industry is even more irrelevant and less structured as compared to religions with far more dangers too.
In this way, modern-day spirituality has refurbished religion and robbed what is scientifically explainable for the sake of economic gain and socio-political influence. Still, if someone insists on walking in the spiritual route to validate their “inner self”, I firmly believe that they should at least remember that the “spiritual” claims and geocentric thoughts aren't much different in terms of evidence, logic, and rationality.
